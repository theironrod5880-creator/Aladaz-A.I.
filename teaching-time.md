
# Teaching Time Series

## Lesson 1: Time Belongs to God
Who decided that a week has seven days? 

Nobody voted on it. No king decreed it. Look up at the sky and you can find the year — the sun makes that. You can find the month — the moon makes that. But nothing up there, nothing in the sky at all, makes a week. And yet every nation on earth, for thousands of years, has counted its days in sevens.

Where did the seven come from?

It came from the first page of the most reliable book we have. "In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth." And the very first thing that book does, before it tells us anything else, is measure time. Day one. Day two. Day three. God counts — out loud, in the open, where everyone can check His work.

That is where this little book begins. Not with man's calendars. With God's.

Because here is the thought that runs under everything we are going to study together: time is not random. Time is measured. It is ruled. The same hand that set the sun in the sky set the pattern of the day, the week, the month, and the year — and then, we will find, He used that same pattern, at a size we can hardly take in, to measure the whole of human history. Seven days in a week. Seven years in a cycle. And at the far end of it, a wheel of time so large it takes just under six thousand years to turn.

But we do not start with that wheel. We start small — the way the Bible starts. A single day. Then a week. Then we watch the pattern grow, one honest step at a time, and we check every step against the Scriptures as we go.

[TEACHING BOX: An hourglass beside an open Bible — men have always watched time; only one book claims to tell us who made it.]

Here is our rule for the whole journey, and we will hold it from the first lesson to the last: every number has to answer to the Bible. If we cannot show it in the Scriptures, we will not print it as fact. Where the record holds a hard place, we will show you the hard place and leave it standing in the open. That is how this work has been built for twenty-five years — slowly, carefully, and in the open.

So come with us. Lesson by lesson, we are going to learn how God's clock is built — from one sunset to the whole of time.

And it starts where the Bible starts: with a day that begins, not at midnight — but in the evening.

[BRING IT BACK: Time is measured, not random — and God does the measuring.]

## Lesson 2: The Day
When does a day begin?

Ask the world, and the world says midnight — a line nobody can see, in the middle of the dark, while everyone is asleep. Ask the Bible, and the Bible gives a different answer, and it gives it on the very first day of creation.

"And there was evening, and there was morning — a first day."

Evening first. Then morning. God's day does not begin at midnight. God's day begins at sunset — when the light goes down, the new day has already started. The evening is not the end of the day. The evening is its front door.

We still carry a trace of this in our own speech. The night before a festival, we call it the "eve" — Christmas Eve, New Year's Eve. Eve means evening. Even our language remembers that the holy day starts the night before.

[TEACHING BOX: Sunset over an open field — the day ending, and the new day beginning, in the same moment.]

Now hold this simple fact in your hand, because we will need it again and again: if a day runs from sunset to sunset, then one Bible day can carry two of our calendar dates. A day that begins Friday at sunset ends Saturday at sunset. It touches Friday. It touches Saturday. But it is one day — one whole, unbroken day in God's reckoning.

That is not a trick. That is just how the calendar works when the day starts where God started it. Later in this study, we will meet dates that look like two different days on paper, and they will turn out to be one and the same day, spanning the sunset boundary. When we get there, you will already know why — because you learned it here, on day one, from the first page of Genesis.

So this is our first standard, and we will write it plainly: The Bible day begins at sunset.

[BRING IT BACK: Evening and morning — God's day starts at sunset, so one of His days can stand across two of ours.]

## Lesson 3: The Week
Seven days. Why not five? Why not ten — we have ten fingers; ten would have been the easy number.

But the week is not built on fingers. The week is built on creation.

In the beginning, God worked six days — light, sky, land, sun and moon, birds and fish, animals and man — and on the seventh day, He stopped. Not because He was tired. The Bible is careful about this: He rested to mark the day, to bless it, to set it apart. The seventh day is different from the other six — and that difference is the whole shape of the week.

Six days alike on which we may work. One day unlike them all: rest. That is the pattern — six, and then the seventh set apart. Hold the shape of it in your mind: "seven, with the last one holy." We are going to meet that shape again at sizes that will surprise you — in years, in lifetimes, and in the whole span of history. But it is born here, in the first week.

[TEACHING BOX: Seven stones in a row — six plain, the seventh set apart and catching the light.]

And here is the mystery tucked inside this simple lesson: the week has no astronomy. The day comes from the turning earth. The month comes from the moon. The year comes from the earth's road around the sun. But the week comes from nothing in the sky. No planet, no star, no cycle of nature divides time into sevens. The seven-day week exists for one reason only: because God counted that way in the beginning, and the count has never been lost — not through empires, not through calendar reforms, not through thousands of years. The Romans had eight-day weeks once. They gave them up. The whole world now keeps a rhythm it cannot explain by looking up.

It can only be explained by looking back — to the week when time itself was measured out by the hand that made it.

[BRING IT BACK: Six days of work, the seventh set apart — a pattern with no sky behind it, given at creation, never lost.]

Tomorrow, we stay with that seventh day — and find out what rest was built into time itself.

## Lesson 4: The Sabbath
What is rest doing inside a clock?

That is the question of this lesson. Time, as we usually think of it, is for doing — working, planting, building, traveling. But woven into the very first week God measured, there is a day that produces nothing. The seventh day. The Sabbath.

And God blessed the very day He made it holy. The seventh day, the day of rest, is the one the blessing lands on. Rest, in God's calendar, is not what is left over when the work is done. Rest is built into the design. It has an appointment, and the appointment is kept every seven days, whether we feel rested or not.

Centuries later, that same day was given to Israel as a sign — a weekly reminder that their lives were measured by God and not by Pharaoh, not by the taskmasters, not by the endless demand to make bricks. Six days you work. The seventh, you stop, and you remember whose you are.

[TEACHING BOX: Tools set down at sunset — the work stops because the day says so, not because the work ran out.]

Now, watch the pattern stretch to the size of a year.

Later, we will find a Sabbath for the land — a whole year of rest, arriving every seventh year. We will find a count of Sabbath years — seven sevens of them — and a fiftieth year of release that follows. And at the very top of the pattern, we will find the whole history of man laid out like a week — with a great rest still standing at the end of it, the seventh "day" of a thousand years.

Seven does not stay small. That is the secret this little book is built on: the same pattern, at every size. A day. A week. A year. An age.

But we are walking, not running. Today the lesson is the day itself: rest is holy, rest is scheduled, and rest belongs to God — who took the first one, and blessed it, and gave it to us in sevens.

[BRING IT BACK: The seventh day is blessed and holy — rest is built into time, and the seven-pattern is about to start growing.]

Tomorrow, we look up — at the moon God hung in the sky to count our months.

## Lesson 5: The Month
If the week has no clock in the sky — the month does. God hung it over our heads.

He made the moon to mark the appointed times. That is the Bible's own job description for the moon: it marks the times. Watch it through a single month and you can see it work. A thin crescent, low in the west at sunset — that is the month being born. Night by night it fattens, until it stands full and round, opposite the setting sun. Then it thins again, morning by morning, until it vanishes — and the next crescent starts the count over.

That is why the months of the Bible begin with the new moon. In Israel, watchers stood on the hills at sunset, looking west. When the first silver was sighted and reported, the month was declared — trumpets blew, and the count of days began. No printed calendar. No calculation on paper. The clock was in the sky, and the whole nation set its months by watching it together.

[TEACHING BOX: The moon in its phases across one month — crescent, half, full, and back to crescent.]

Count the moons and you build the year of months: twelve of them, running one after another — twelve months, about three hundred and fifty-four days. We will have more to say about the year in the next lesson. For now, just hold the pieces we have gathered: the day that starts at sunset, the week of seven with its holy seventh, and now the month, counted by the moon.

And keep one small number from this lesson in a corner of your memory, because much later it will matter more than you expect: twelve. Twelve months in the year. The wheel of time loves the number twelve — months in a year, and hours doubled in a day, and far down the road we will find whole ages of history shrinking and growing by twelves — four hundred and ninety years that hold five thousand, eight hundred and eighty months, as if the great wheel were the small one, turned up twelve times.

That is a promise for a later lesson. Today, the moon is enough: God put a clock in the night sky, and taught His people to read it.

[BRING IT BACK: The moon marks the months — twelve of them make a year of months, and twelve is a number the wheel will use again.]

Tomorrow: the year itself — and the surprise of when it begins.

## Lesson 6: The Year
When does the year begin?

The world says January — the dead of winter, in the northern lands: snow on the ground, nothing growing, a strange place to start anything. But the world's calendar is a patchwork of kings and emperors. The Bible's calendar is older, and it answers differently.

In God's reckoning, the year begins in autumn. The seventh month of the sacred count — the month called Tishri — opens the civil year, around September and October by our reckoning: the harvest gathered in, the rains about to come, the old year closed and the new one opened at the turning of the seasons. The great autumn feasts stand at that gate — Trumpets, Atonement, Booths — as if God posted His most solemn appointments right at the year's front door.

[TEACHING BOX: Autumn fields with the harvest gathered in — the year turning over at the season of ingathering.]

Now, here is a detail worth holding carefully, because it confuses many readers: the Bible actually carries two counts at once. When God brought Israel out of Egypt, he moved the religious count — this month will be the first month of the year for you — so the feast year begins in the spring, with Passover. But the civil and agricultural year — the year for counting reigns, and Sabbaths, and Jubilees — still begins in autumn. Two counts, one calendar, each with its own work. It is no contradiction; it is a clock with two dials.

And here is why this standard matters to everything ahead of us: the great timeline we are building toward the whole 5,880-year wheel is counted from an autumn beginning. The count of man's history under the Fall opens in autumn, in the year 3967 B.C.E., when the sixth creative day drew to its close. When we arrive at that date, months from now in our lessons, you will not have to stop and ask why the year starts where it starts. You will already hold the standard, because we set it here, in Part 1, where it belongs.

So write it beside the first one: the day begins at sunset; the year begins in autumn.

[BRING IT BACK: God's year opens in autumn, at the ingathering — and the whole timeline ahead is counted from an autumn start.]

## Lesson 7: Counting Honestly
There is a year that does not exist. And it has broken more timelines than any enemy of the truth ever could.

The year is the year zero.

When we count backward from our era into the years before it, the count runs like this: 3 B.C.E., 2 B.C.E., 1 B.C.E. — and then, not zero, but straight to 1 C.E. There is no year zero. The first century before Christ and the first century after him stand back to back, with no year in between.

Sounds harmless. But watch what it does to arithmetic. Suppose an event happened in 2 B.C.E., and you want the years from then until 2 C.E. Count it carelessly — two plus two — and you will say four years. Count it honestly, year by year: 2 B.C.E. to 1 B.C.E. is one; 1 B.C.E. to 1 C.E. is two; 1 C.E. to 2 C.E. is three. Three years, not four. The missing zero steals one year out of every careless calculation that crosses the line — and the line is crossed by every timeline that runs from the Bible's world into ours.

[TEACHING BOX: A fence with posts — we count the posts, and when the posts run out, we do not invent one more.]

Here is how the great sum of this book is built, and you can check it yourself when we get there: from the Fall in 3967 B.C.E. to 1914 C.E. is five thousand, eight hundred and eighty years — 3,967 plus 1,914, minus one. That minus one is the missing year zero, honestly confessed in the open. No silent corrections. No hidden adjustments. The math shows its work, because the math has nothing to hide.

And that is the spirit of this whole study, and the right place to close Part 1. We are serious about accuracy and facts. Every date in this work has been checked and rechecked against the Bible. We use every tool God has provided, including modern ones, to bring this message to the world — but the numbers must answer to the Scriptures, or they do not get printed.

Let us gather the standards of Part 1 in one place, because all of them ride with us from here:

The day begins at sunset. The week is seven, with the seventh set apart. The moon counts the months — twelve to a year. The year begins in autumn. And there is no year zero.

Five small standards. In Part 2, we put them to work — on the feasts of Israel, and on a count of seven times seven that ends, one day beyond, at fifty.

[BRING IT BACK: No year zero — 3,967 + 1,914 - 1 = 5,880. Count honestly, in the open, and the wheel will turn true.]

Your King, Jesus Christ, will be arriving soon.
